COR certification in BC: what small employers need to know.

October 21, 2026  ·  BC Safety Docs  ·  7 min read

If you run a small business in BC and keep hearing about COR, here is the plain version: what it is, whether you have to have it, how you get it, and what to do first. Everything below comes from WorkSafeBC's own pages and from the certifying partners' published rules, with the sources listed at the end.

What COR is

COR stands for Certificate of Recognition. WorkSafeBC describes it as a voluntary employer certification program, run through its Partners Program. It recognizes employers who go beyond what the law requires, put an effective occupational health and safety management system in place, and pass a certification audit.

Is COR required?

No. WorkSafeBC calls the program voluntary: employers choose to take part by registering with a certifying partner. WorkSafeBC does say COR can give you an advantage when it comes to work contracts, so it is worth asking the customers and prime contractors you work for whether they look for it. COR also comes with an annual financial incentive (more on that below).

How certification works

WorkSafeBC lays out the process in a few steps:

  1. You register with the certifying partner for your industry.
  2. You build or update your health and safety management system.
  3. A qualified auditor audits it.
  4. Your partner reviews the audit report for quality.
  5. If you pass, WorkSafeBC issues the certificate and your partner awards it.
  6. The certificate is typically valid for three years, with annual maintenance audits in between.
  7. After three years you pass a re-certification audit.

WorkSafeBC charges no fee to take part, but there can be costs for training, partner requirements, audits and putting the system in place. Fees and requirements differ by partner, so ask yours.

Who certifies you

COR is delivered by certifying partners, usually industry health and safety associations that have contracts with WorkSafeBC. You are matched by your industry:

  • AgSafe: agriculture
  • BC Construction Safety Alliance (BCCSA): construction
  • BC Forest Safety Council: forestry
  • BC Municipal Safety Association: local government
  • Energy Safety Canada: upstream oil and gas
  • go2HR: tourism and hospitality
  • Manufacturing Safety Alliance of BC: manufacturing and food processing
  • SafetyDriven: trucking, transportation, warehousing, shipping and logistics

If none of those fits your industry, WorkSafeBC says it will align you with a partner in a related sector. Its example is a window manufacturer that installs on construction sites, which counts as construction.

What counts as a small employer

There is no single WorkSafeBC definition, so check your own partner. From the partners' published pages (checked October 9, 2026):

  • BCCSA: Small COR is for 19 or fewer employees and can use your own trained internal auditor. With 20 or more, certification needs an external auditor.
  • AgSafe: small employers (19 or fewer full-time employees) can use a trained internal auditor or an AgSafe-approved external auditor.
  • go2HR: its small-employer program, SECOR, is for fewer than 20 employees. Its terms and FAQ describe internal auditors for small employers, but its website audit page says external, so confirm with go2HR.
  • BC Forest Safety Council: small employers (2 to 19 workers) use an internal auditor.
  • Energy Safety Canada: COR is for 11 or more employees and SECOR for 10 or fewer.

An internal auditor is normally a trained employee of your own company, and the partners set the training rules, so ask yours before you plan around it.

What the auditor looks at

WorkSafeBC says the audit reviews your health and safety management system. It lists these parts: management leadership and commitment; safe work procedures; training and instruction; identifying hazards and managing risk; inspections; incident investigation; program administration; joint health and safety committee or representatives; health and safety programs; and the system audit itself. How much of each applies depends on the size and hazards of your workplace.

Each partner publishes its own audit tool, and the structure differs. A common way to look at a small employer's written program is as 13 elements: policy, hazard assessment and control, safe work practices, safe job procedures, company rules, personal protective equipment, preventive maintenance, training and communication, workplace inspections, investigations and reporting, emergency preparedness, statistics and records, and legislation. Treat that as a readiness view, not an audit score. Energy Safety Canada, for example, uses 10 elements, so check your partner's own tool.

What score you need

The partners we checked publish the same pass mark: 80% overall and at least 50% in every element (stated by BCCSA, AgSafe, go2HR, Energy Safety Canada and the BC Forest Safety Council). That means one empty element can fail an audit that is strong everywhere else. The WorkSafeBC pages we read say only that you must pass, so confirm the figure with your partner.

How much record history you need

Auditors want to see that the system has been running, not just written down. BCCSA says there should be at least 6 months of supporting evidence, with 12 months recommended. The BC Forest Safety Council's audit covers the 12 months before the audit date. Energy Safety Canada advises 12 months of documentation for its small-employer program. Some partners publish no minimum at all, so ask yours. The practical point is the same everywhere: start keeping records now, because that clock cannot be skipped later.

What COR can pay

Certified employers can earn an annual WorkSafeBC incentive: 10% of base assessment premiums for each classification unit in your COR, with a minimum of the lesser of $1,000 or 75% of the premiums you paid. WorkSafeBC considers it in the second quarter of the year after certification and expects to credit your account by the end of June. You need a valid certificate and no exceptions, such as late payroll reporting or a health and safety conviction. Want a number for your business? Try the free COR incentive calculator.

Where to start

  1. Find your certifying partner using the lookup on WorkSafeBC's certifying partners page.
  2. Ask them three questions: what audit options apply to a business your size (can you use a trained internal auditor?), how much record history they expect before the first audit, and what it costs.
  3. See where you stand. The free COR gap check takes two minutes and shows which elements are in place, which are missing, and which documents close each gap.
  4. Build the written program. Several of the documents are free templates, including the OHS policy statement, JSA, daily FLHA, safe work procedure, toolbox talk record, incident investigation report and emergency response plan. The rest are in the COR Small-Employer Kit (18 editable documents, $279 CAD).
  5. Start keeping records of inspections, training, meetings and incidents so you have a history to show.

Documents are not certification. Templates and the kit give you the written program an audit looks for. Certification is awarded through your certifying partner after a passed audit.
